Numbers Without the Headache

You don't need to become a spreadsheet person. You just need to hand off the sorting so you can get to the decision faster.

The Story

The expense sheet nobody wants to open

Picture a spreadsheet that's been growing for three months — rows added whenever someone remembered, categories that don't match, a few duplicate entries, totals nobody trusts anymore. Everyone's got one of these. A budget, an inventory list, a client tracker.

The instinct is to avoid it until it becomes an emergency. But here's the thing — the sheet isn't actually hard. It's just messy, and untangling mess by hand is slow and tedious in a way that makes people procrastinate.

The fix isn't a fancier spreadsheet tool. It's handing the untangling part to AI, and keeping the actual decision — what do I do about this — for yourself.

The Lesson

Name the decision, not just the data

Don't just say "clean this up." Say what you're actually trying to decide — "am I overspending in one category," "which clients haven't paid," "what's actually left in the budget." That target decision tells it how to organize everything.

The move: paste the messy data in as-is, name the real decision you're trying to make, and ask it to organize toward that — flagging anything that looks off.

Try It Now

Vague vs. specific

Vague "Clean up this spreadsheet." [pastes messy data]

It'll tidy formatting, maybe. It has no idea what "clean" should serve.

Specific "Here's my last 3 months of expenses, pasted as-is, categories are inconsistent: [paste data]. I need to know if I'm overspending on supplies compared to the first month. Organize this by category and month, flag any duplicate entries, and tell me the trend on supplies specifically."

Now it's not just tidying — it's answering the actual question you had.

Your Challenge Today

Untangle one real mess

Find the one spreadsheet or data pile you've been avoiding. Paste it in as-is, name the real decision you need to make from it, and let it organize toward that answer.

⏱ Under an hour
Reflection

Before you move on

  • What surprised you about what it flagged in your data?
  • What worked — did naming the decision change what it gave you back?
  • What would you do differently — what decision would you name more precisely next time?
