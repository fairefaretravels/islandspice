## 2. STORY

Meet Priya. Priya runs a small online shop, and she's got a spreadsheet of every order from the last six months — except it's a mess. Some rows are duplicated. Some customer names are spelled three different ways. She has no idea, at a glance, what her best-selling product actually is.

She tried asking AI to "analyze my spreadsheet." She got back a paragraph of generic advice about pivot tables. Not wrong. Not useful either — she still had the same messy sheet in front of her.

The shift happened when Priya stopped asking for generic analysis and started describing exactly what a "clean" version would let her see — and exactly what question she was actually trying to answer: which products were actually making her money.

Same spreadsheet. Same AI. Completely different outcome — because Priya finally told it what "clean" and "useful" meant to her.

---

## 3. LESSON

Still the same rule, new territory:

**Vague in, vague out. Specific in, useful out.**

For spreadsheets, "specific" means three things:

1. **Name what a clean row should look like.** Not "fix my data" — say what should be in each column, and what a duplicate or an error actually looks like in your sheet.

2. **Name the real question you're trying to answer.** Not "analyze this" — say what you actually want to know. Best seller? Slowest month? Which client hasn't paid? AI can't guess your goal — you have to say it.

3. **Ask for a summary, not just a fix.** A clean table is step one. The real value is a short answer: "here's what changed, and here's what it tells you."

Name the row, name the question, ask for the takeaway. That's the whole skill, and it works whether it's ten rows or ten thousand.

---

## 4. DEMO

Let's do this on a real mess.

**Step one — the vague version.**

> "Clean up my spreadsheet."

*(Pause.)*

Generic tips about formulas and formatting. No idea what "clean" means for this specific sheet, because we never told it.

**Step two — let's fix it.**

> "Here's a sample of my order data: [paste rows]. Each row should have one order — no duplicates, dates in MM/DD/YYYY, customer names standardized. I want to know which three products brought in the most revenue over the last six months, and whether any month stands out. Give me the cleaned data and a short summary of what you find."

*(Pause.)*

Now look — real cleanup rules, a real question answered, and a plain-English takeaway instead of a wall of numbers.

**Step three — building your standing data-cleanup prompt.**

Here's today's project, the reusable version:

> "From now on, when I paste in messy data, clean it using these rules: [your real rules — no duplicates, standardized names, consistent dates, whatever applies]. Then answer this question based on the data: [your real recurring question — best seller, biggest expense, slowest week]. Always end with a two-sentence plain-English takeaway."

Save it. Run your actual messy sheet through it before tomorrow.
