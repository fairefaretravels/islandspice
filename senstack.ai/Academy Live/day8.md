## 2. STORY

Meet Daniel. Daniel needed to write a proposal for his manager — a real one, with real stakes, that had been sitting untouched in his drafts folder for two weeks. Every time he opened it, he typed a sentence, deleted it, and closed the laptop.

He finally tried AI. He typed "write me a business proposal." He got back three pages of generic filler — no mention of his actual team, his actual budget, his actual ask. It read like it was written for nobody in particular. Because it was.

The fix wasn't a better AI. It was Daniel finally naming who was going to read it, what he actually needed them to say yes to, and the three points that had to land for that "yes" to happen.

Once he did that, the first draft that came back wasn't perfect — but it was real. It was something he could edit instead of something he had to invent from a blank page. That's the whole difference.

---

## 3. LESSON

Same rule, new page:

**Vague in, vague out. Specific in, useful out.**

For documents, "specific" means three things:

1. **Name your reader.** Not "write a proposal" — say who's reading it, and what they already know or don't know.

2. **Name the ask.** What do you actually need them to do, approve, or understand by the end of it?

3. **Name the three points that have to land.** Not everything — the three things that, if the reader remembers nothing else, they remember these.

Reader, ask, three points. That's the whole skill, and it turns a blank page into an editing job instead of an invention job.

---

## 4. DEMO

Let's build one live.

**Step one — the vague version.**

> "Write me a proposal for a new project."

*(Pause.)*

Generic. Could be about anything. Reads like it was written for no one, because it was.

**Step two — let's fix it.**

> "Write a one-page proposal for my manager, who already knows our team but hasn't seen the numbers. I need approval to hire one part-time contractor for the next quarter. The three points that have to land: this reduces our current backlog by half, it costs less than the overtime we're already paying, and it's a three-month trial, not a permanent hire. Keep it direct, no fluff, one page."

*(Pause.)*

Look at that. A real draft, built for a real reader, making a real case — not generic filler.

**Step three — building your standing document-drafting prompt.**

Here's the reusable version for today's project:

> "From now on, when I ask you to draft a document, first ask me who's reading it, what I need them to do, and the three points that have to land — if I haven't already told you. Then write a direct first draft, no fluff, structured around those three points."
